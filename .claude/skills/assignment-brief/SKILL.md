# Assignment brief to checklist

Briefs bury the expensive parts. A requirement worth ten points can sit in one
sentence in the middle of a paragraph while the formatting instructions get a
whole section. This pulls out what costs points and puts it first.

Works on a file path, a pasted brief, or a screenshot of a Canvas page.

## Mode 1: break down the brief

Write to `~/Desktop/assignments/<COURSE>/<due-date>-<short-name>.md`, creating
folders as needed. Ask for the course code if the brief does not say it.

Sections, in this order:

1. **Due.** Date, time, and exactly where it gets turned in — the specific form,
   portal or address. Quote the brief. A deliverable submitted to the wrong place
   scores the same as one never written.
2. **Hard requirements.** Sentences of the form "you must", "each submission
   has to", "no credit without". These often carry no points of their own but
   gate everything else, so they are the easiest points in the document to lose.
   Every one gets its own line.
3. **Every graded item, with its points.** Reproduce the rubric as a table. Never
   invent or estimate a point value. If the brief does not weight something, write
   `unweighted` rather than a guess.
4. **Caps and penalties.** Conditions that limit a score regardless of quality
   ("caps this question at 30", "costs you 10 points", late policy). These are
   what turn a good submission into a mediocre grade.
5. **Excellent versus the row below.** For each graded dimension, the specific
   difference between top marks and the next band down. That difference is the
   entire to-do list for the grade, and it is usually one or two concrete things,
   not "try harder".
6. **Checklist.** One `- [ ]` per deliverable, ordered by points at risk,
   highest first. Not in the order the brief happens to mention them.
7. **Ask about.** Anything genuinely ambiguous. Keep this short and specific — a
   question worth a professor's time, not a question the brief already answers.

## Mode 2: check before submitting

When Yohan says he is close to done, or hands over work plus the brief: go
through the checklist and mark each item `done`, `partial` or `missing`, with the
evidence for anything marked done — the URL, the file path, the actual value he
will paste. Then state the total points currently at risk, and put the missing
items in the order he should fix them given the time left.

Say the number of points at risk out loud in the terminal. A number moves people;
a list of tasks does not.

## Rules

- **Quote, don't paraphrase, anything with points or a deadline attached.** His
  professor's exact words are what the grade is measured against.
- **Never invent a point value, a deadline or a requirement.** If the brief is
  silent, say it is silent.
- **Flag self-contradictions** rather than resolving them silently. Briefs written
  the morning they are handed out contain real mistakes, and the professor would
  rather hear about it than have it guessed at.
- **An empty box scores zero and a thin answer scores partial**, so never leave a
  required field blank in a draft — put the best available answer in and mark it
  `[thin]` for him to improve.
